# Gate 6 UX Recovery and Design Plan

## Status

Approved direction recorded on 11 August 2026.

This plan replaces the visual-first Gate 6 attempt. It does not approve an interface, visual direction, prototype or production change.

## Why Gate 6 was reset

The initial Gate 6 visual exploration began with representative high-fidelity screens before defining the complete product model, lifecycle, navigation, state scope, content priority, interaction sequence and responsive reading order.

The resulting screens appeared polished but did not form a durable product:

- navigation was organized around one pilot rather than the complete adoption lifecycle;
- pilot-specific areas appeared to be permanent product navigation;
- current posture, evidence sufficiency, decision result and work authority were visually combined without showing their different scopes;
- core consequences were separated from the response that caused them;
- a permanent right-side evaluation panel made one decision look like an ongoing global state;
- the designs did not explain how the workspace operates after the pilot;
- mobile reading order and lifecycle continuity were not established before desktop composition.

The three generated visual options are rejected explorations. They are not approved references.

## Corrected design objective

Design a human-centered product that helps an enterprise move from understanding the AI-adoption problem through readiness, controlled piloting, complete-SDLC evaluation, remediation, accountable expansion or restriction, and ongoing operation.

The product must always help a user understand:

- why the product exists;
- where the organization is in the adoption journey;
- what exact scope is being considered;
- what decision is required now;
- who is accountable;
- what evidence is required;
- what the evidence supports;
- what may continue;
- what must stop;
- what must be remediated;
- who may authorize the next action;
- what happens after the current pilot or decision.

## 1. Define the connected product surfaces

### Public playbook

The public experience must explain the problem, audience, full journey, participants, SDLC coverage, evidence discipline and starting choices before requesting organizational information.

It must remain useful without entering the workspace.

### Adoption workspace

The workspace applies the guidance to an adoption initiative. It must support multiple pilots and decisions over time.

It must not be structured as a pilot-only workbook, a questionnaire, a maturity test or a page-completion wizard.

## 2. Define the durable product model

Gate 6 must specify the relationships among:

- organization;
- adoption initiative;
- current AI participation posture;
- journey stage;
- pilot or adoption increment;
- material decision;
- evidence record;
- risk;
- hard stop;
- remediation;
- approval;
- evaluation;
- leadership decision;
- report.

Each displayed status must name its scope. A single general status must not combine organization posture, pilot readiness, evidence sufficiency, decision result and authority.

## 3. Map the complete lifecycle

The experience model must cover:

1. Understand the adoption problem.
2. Assess organizational foundations and baseline readiness.
3. Select a suitable opportunity.
4. Design a bounded adoption increment.
5. Prepare and decide whether it may launch.
6. Run under approved boundaries.
7. Capture evidence across the complete affected SDLC and total cost.
8. Evaluate tangible net value and governing risks.
9. Continue, restrict, remediate, expand or stop.
10. Repeat with another increment or move toward governed integration.
11. Operate, monitor and improve while preserving stop authority.

The model must include alternate paths:

- blocked before launch;
- approved with conditions;
- stopped during execution;
- evidence incomplete;
- local implementation improvement with negative net value;
- remediation and reassessment;
- controlled expansion;
- leadership decision to stop further investment.

## 4. Design durable navigation

Navigation must reflect stable product concepts rather than one representative scenario.

The design work must evaluate a structure that includes:

- initiative overview;
- adoption journey;
- decisions;
- pilots or increments;
- evidence;
- risks and remediation;
- leadership decisions;
- reports.

Pilot-specific navigation may appear only inside a selected pilot and must retain a clear parent relationship to the adoption initiative.

Role and SDLC views remain secondary lenses over shared guidance and records. They must not create separate sources of truth.

The selected navigation must let a representative user answer:

- Where am I?
- What scope am I working on?
- What is the parent adoption initiative?
- What decision is required?
- What happens after this pilot?
- Where is an earlier decision or evidence record?
- Where would another pilot be evaluated?
- Where would leadership review the initiative as a whole?

The information structure must be approved before choosing a sidebar, top navigation or another presentation pattern.

## 5. Establish page-level content priority

Every material decision experience must preserve this reasoning sequence:

1. Decision and exact scope
2. Why the decision matters
3. Accountable owner and required authority
4. Evidence requirement
5. Response and supporting evidence
6. Evidence interpretation
7. Immediate hard stop when triggered
8. What may continue
9. What must stop
10. Required remediation
11. Next accountable decision
12. Decision history and secondary method detail

Core consequences must appear in the same reading sequence as the response that caused them. They must not be hidden in a report or placed only in a detached side panel.

Examples that are useful and not sufficient must be available at the point where evidence or a written response is requested.

## 6. Establish visual hierarchy before visual style

For a material decision page:

- The current decision and immediate consequence are primary.
- Evidence requirements, response and interpretation are secondary.
- Examples, definitions and history support the task.
- Initiative, pilot, journey stage and accountable owner provide persistent context.
- A hard stop interrupts the normal hierarchy and receives immediate attention.

Color, typography, spacing, surface treatment and navigation placement may reinforce this hierarchy only after the hierarchy is approved.

## 7. Define responsive reading order

Mobile reading order must be defined before desktop rails or multiple columns are introduced.

Critical meaning must not depend on:

- a permanently open sidebar;
- a sticky consequence rail;
- a wide table;
- hover;
- horizontal scrolling;
- small status badges;
- page-progress controls.

A desktop secondary rail is permitted only when its contents remain secondary and retain meaning when stacked later in the mobile reading order.

## 8. Produce structural wireframes

Create low-fidelity, responsive wireframes for:

- public orientation;
- adoption initiative overview;
- readiness decision;
- hard-stop and remediation;
- pilot evaluation;
- leadership decision;
- post-pilot continuation, restriction, expansion or stop.

The wireframes must show navigation, content order, state scope, actions and transitions. They must avoid decorative visual styling so structural issues can be resolved first.

## 9. Review in bounded checkpoints

### Checkpoint 6.1: Product model and lifecycle

Approve product surfaces, durable information objects, state scope, lifecycle and alternate paths.

### Checkpoint 6.2: Navigation and screen responsibilities

Approve global navigation, local pilot navigation, direct destinations, screen responsibilities and movement across pre-pilot, pilot and post-pilot work.

### Checkpoint 6.3: Content and interaction hierarchy

Approve page-level content priority, response behavior, evidence guidance, interpretation, hard stops, remediation, authority boundaries and leadership outputs.

### Checkpoint 6.4: Responsive structural wireframes

Approve desktop and mobile wireframes for the representative path. This checkpoint approves structure, not branding.

### Checkpoint 6.5: Visual direction and system

Compare visual directions built from the same approved wireframes. Variants may change visual treatment but must not introduce different information architectures or content priorities.

### Checkpoint 6.6: Working vertical slice

Build the approved orientation, readiness, remediation, later-scenario transition, completed-pilot evaluation and leadership-output sequence.

### Checkpoint 6.7: Human validation

Validate comprehension, navigation, authority boundaries, hard stops, net-value reasoning, leadership decisions, mobile usability and keyboard operation.

If representative participants are unavailable, the honest result is ready for human validation, not validated.

## 10. Build the Gate 6 vertical slice

The working slice must let a representative user:

1. Understand why the product exists.
2. Choose Assess readiness with an informed expectation.
3. Enter an adoption initiative and exact pilot scope.
4. Review a material readiness decision.
5. Provide representative evidence.
6. Encounter an immediate hard stop.
7. Distinguish permitted planning and remediation from prohibited real work.
8. Inspect remediation and understand that completion does not restore authority.
9. Move through an explicit later-scenario boundary.
10. Evaluate complete-SDLC and total-cost evidence.
11. See that faster implementation did not produce positive net value.
12. Record the accountable restriction to Assisted Work.
13. Review the leadership choice.
14. Produce a readable provisional leadership output.

The prototype may use disposable state. It must remain isolated from production and must not decide the Gate 7 platform.

## Gate 6 definition of ready

Gate 6 is ready for product-owner completion review only when it has:

- an approved product model;
- an approved lifecycle and alternate paths;
- durable navigation that works beyond one pilot;
- explicit state scope;
- approved content and interaction hierarchy;
- responsive structural wireframes;
- an approved visual system;
- a working readiness-to-evaluation vertical slice;
- a readable leadership output;
- a human-validation script;
- an explicit validation result.

A successful build alone is not a validated experience.
