# Gate 5 Experience 1: Understand the Purpose and Choose a Starting Point

## Status and scope

This is the first end-user content experience required by [Gate 5](https://github.com/scott-wojan/enterprise-ai-sdlc/issues/140).

It defines exact reader-facing content for public orientation and the transition into applying the playbook. It does not define visual styling, page layout, software behavior, persistence, authentication or production implementation. It does not change the current production site.

The intended reader is a technology or delivery leader who has been asked to establish an enterprise approach to AI-assisted software delivery and does not yet know what must be decided or where to begin.

## Intended outcome

After this experience, a reader should be able to explain:

- why the product exists;
- which organizational problems it addresses;
- why adopting an AI tool is not the same as adopting AI responsibly;
- what the complete adoption journey covers;
- which software-delivery activities are included;
- who needs to participate;
- how evidence, risk and stopping protect the organization;
- which starting choice fits the reader's current situation;
- what that choice will produce and what is needed before beginning.

The reader is not asked to create a workspace, select a role, complete a readiness check or enter organizational information until this orientation is complete.

### Who this product is for

**Heading**

Use this when your organization needs an adoption decision, not another tool demonstration

**Copy**

This product is for people who need to establish, operate, evaluate or govern AI-assisted software delivery across an enterprise or consulting engagement.

It is relevant when you:

- have been asked to define how your organization should adopt AI in software delivery;
- need to replace disconnected team experiments with shared decisions and boundaries;
- are preparing or reviewing a controlled adoption trial;
- own delivery, architecture, engineering, quality, security, release, operations, risk, finance or workforce decisions affected by AI;
- need to show leadership whether adoption created tangible value and what should happen next;
- need to expand useful practices without granting uncontrolled authority.

This is not a guide for selecting an AI vendor, configuring a specific product or building AI features for customers. It does not replace broader legal, regulatory or enterprise AI governance that applies outside software delivery.

### Why this product exists

**Heading**

Why this product exists

**Copy**

AI adoption often begins with a tool purchase, a leadership directive or individual experimentation. Those activities can create useful learning, but they do not answer the enterprise questions that determine whether AI-assisted delivery can be trusted, supported and justified.

An organization still needs to decide which work is appropriate, who is accountable, what AI may access, what AI may do, how its output will be checked, what happens when something goes wrong and what evidence will show whether the investment helped.

Without those decisions, teams can move at different speeds under different assumptions. Review and support work may move downstream without being measured. Sensitive information or excessive authority may be exposed. Local coding speed may be reported as success while defects, rework, operational burden or total cost become worse.

This product gives organizations one decision path for introducing AI into software delivery, learning from real work and expanding only when the complete evidence supports it.

### Challenges this product helps address

**Heading**

What challenges does this product help with?

**Introductory copy**

The playbook helps an organization answer the questions that a tool rollout cannot answer on its own.

**Challenge 1**

What type of work is appropriate to delegate to AI?

Different software-delivery activities require different context, judgment, validation and authority. Work that is suitable for drafting or analysis may be unsuitable for direct change or execution.

**Challenge 2**

Who remains accountable for AI output?

AI can support a decision or produce work, but an identifiable person must remain able to review it, reject it, stop the work and answer for the result.

**Challenge 3**

What information and tools should AI be allowed to access?

Access must be limited to the data, repositories, systems, tools and actions required for the approved work. Tool capability does not equal organizational permission.

**Challenge 4**

How will work produced with AI be validated and approved?

The organization must decide which tests, checks, reviews and human approvals apply before an AI-assisted output can influence managed delivery or change a system.

**Challenge 5**

What evidence will show whether AI adoption is helping?

Evidence must cover the complete affected delivery path and total material cost. Faster implementation is not a win if review, testing, rework, defects, support, operational work or cost becomes worse.

**Challenge 6**

What happens when AI output is wrong or the team loses confidence in adopting it?

People need a safe way to report problems. The organization needs clear conditions for restricting, pausing or stopping work and a defined path for remediation and reassessment.

**Challenge 7**

Who decides whether to continue investing in AI-assisted software delivery?

Leadership owns the investment decision, but security, legal, financial, release, service and other accountable owners still decide within their areas of authority. Continued investment cannot erase an unresolved stop condition.

### What the organization can decide and produce

**Heading**

Turn adoption pressure into decisions people can act on

**Copy**

This product is not a reading checklist and it does not produce a generic maturity score. It helps an organization create a connected set of decisions and working records.

You can use it to produce:

- a clear statement of why the organization is considering AI and who owns the decision;
- a readiness position that identifies what is ready, what is restricted and what must be fixed;
- a record of use cases that were selected, narrowed, deferred or rejected;
- a controlled first-increment plan with explicit scope, authority, validation, evidence and stop conditions;
- a visible record of risks, missing evidence, remediation and current restrictions;
- an assessment of tangible value across the complete affected delivery path and total cost;
- a leadership decision to continue, restrict, remediate, evaluate one bounded expansion or stop;
- an operating plan for practices that earn continued use.

Each output is assembled from named decisions and evidence. You do not begin with a blank workbook and figure out the method yourself.

### What responsible adoption does not mean

**Heading**

Responsible adoption is not maximum automation

**Copy**

Responsible adoption does not mean:

- buying an AI tool and requiring teams to use it;
- treating generated code, completed stories or suggestion acceptance as proof of value;
- allowing every team, repository or work type to use the same level of AI authority;
- assuming that faster implementation outweighs slower review, more defects or higher support cost;
- transferring accountability from people to an AI system;
- bypassing existing engineering, security, release or operational controls;
- expanding because a tool can perform more actions;
- treating every problem as something AI should solve.

An organization may decide that analysis and drafting are the right long-term use for a particular scope. Greater authority is appropriate only when the work, controls, evidence and expected value justify it.

### The complete adoption journey

**Heading**

The adoption journey

**Introductory copy**

The journey is a sequence of decisions, not a race toward greater autonomy. Organizations may enter with work already underway, but a later-stage gap can require a return to an earlier decision. A pilot is one adoption increment, not the entire journey.

**Stage 1: Understand and align**

Decide why the organization is considering AI, what problem it is trying to address, who owns the investment and who can stop the work.

Result: an adoption intent and ownership decision.

Return or stop when: the purpose is vague, success has been predetermined or nobody has authority to own and stop the work.

**Stage 2: Assess readiness and establish baselines**

Determine whether the people, engineering practices, context, controls, evidence and measurement needed for the proposed work exist.

Result: a readiness position, baseline, restrictions and remediation plan.

Return or stop when: required controls cannot operate, a meaningful comparison cannot be established or material downstream effects and costs cannot be observed.

**Stage 3: Select opportunities**

Choose work where AI may create value and reject work that is unsuitable, uncontrolled, unmeasurable or not worth pursuing.

Result: a use-case selection and rejection record.

Return or stop when: the outcome is vague, the work requires unacceptable authority or the result cannot be checked reliably.

**Stage 4: Design the first adoption increment**

Define the exact work, participants, boundaries, controls, evidence, support, cost limits and decision date for a controlled trial.

Result: an approved first-increment charter and evidence plan.

Return or stop when: the scope is not bounded, people cannot halt the work or the organization cannot contain and recover from failure.

**Stage 5: Prepare and run**

Test the boundaries and controls, prepare participants, perform approved work and capture both favorable and adverse evidence.

Result: operating decisions, health reports, incident records and a complete evidence package.

Return or stop when: authority is exceeded, required checks fail, evidence becomes unreliable or material harm is occurring.

**Stage 6: Evaluate and decide**

Compare the complete affected delivery path with the baseline, include total material cost and decide what the evidence supports.

Result: a tangible-value assessment, decision memorandum and leadership briefing.

Return or stop when: evidence is incomplete, the result is worse overall or a success claim depends only on local speed or usage.

**Stage 7: Expand incrementally**

Consider changing one bounded dimension, such as one team, work type, repository, data class, tool or level of authority.

Result: an approved or rejected next-increment plan.

Return or stop when: several changes are bundled together, prior value was not demonstrated or support and controls cannot scale.

**Stage 8: Govern and integrate**

Turn practices that have earned continued use into repeatable workflows with durable ownership, controls, evidence and support.

Result: a governed workflow and operating record.

Return or stop when: results depend on a few exceptional people, controls are unsustainable or material costs remain unknown.

**Stage 9: Operate and improve**

Reassess value, cost, controls, incidents, participant experience and changing tools or obligations. Improve, restrict or retire practices as conditions change.

Result: a current operating decision and improvement or retirement plan.

Return or stop when: value declines, authority expires, controls drift or the practice no longer serves a business need.

### Who needs to participate and why

**Heading**

AI adoption is a shared enterprise decision

**Introductory copy**

One person can coordinate adoption, but one person cannot supply every decision, control and piece of evidence. Participation should match the exact work and risk. Not every role attends every meeting, but every affected decision needs an authorized owner.

**Leadership and adoption ownership**

An executive sponsor owns enterprise intent and continued investment. An adoption owner coordinates the journey, keeps the current decision visible and ensures work stops when governing conditions no longer hold.

**Product and delivery**

Product and delivery leaders define the problem worth solving, the affected work and the outcome that matters. Delivery participants report what happened in practice, including rework, friction and failure.

**Engineering, architecture and platform**

These participants determine whether repositories, systems, context, tools, permissions, tests and delivery environments can support the proposed use.

**Quality, security, legal and compliance**

These participants define and verify the checks, restrictions and category decisions required for the exact work, data and consequences.

**Release, service and operations**

These participants account for deployment, reliability, recovery, incidents, support effort and production effects that may otherwise remain invisible.

**Measurement and finance**

These participants establish a trustworthy comparison, include transferred work and total material cost and challenge unsupported savings or productivity claims.

**Independent review**

Someone other than the people performing or sponsoring the work must challenge the evidence before the organization declares success or expands.

### How evidence and decision discipline protect the organization

**Heading**

Prove the result across the complete delivery system

**Copy**

AI activity is easy to count. Enterprise value is harder to prove.

The playbook protects the organization by applying five rules:

1. Define the outcome and comparison before evaluative work begins.
2. Measure the complete affected delivery path, not only the activity where AI was used.
3. Include total material cost, including tools, tokens, integration, training, review, controls, rework, support and incidents.
4. Keep quality, security, operations, maintainability, team experience and economics visible as separate results. Do not average them into one favorable score.
5. Preserve missing, adverse and dissenting evidence so leadership can see what is known, what is unknown and what became worse.

**Example**

A team completes implementation work faster with AI, but reviewers spend more time correcting it, defects increase and production support effort rises. That is not a productivity win. The complete delivery system became more expensive or less effective even though one activity became faster.

If material downstream effort or total cost cannot be established, the honest conclusion is that value has not been demonstrated. That conclusion prevents an unsupported claim. It does not prevent the organization from fixing its evidence approach or running a separately approved learning exercise.

**Evidence action helper text**

### Risk, stopping and remediation

**Heading**

Stopping protects people, systems and investment

**Copy**

A stop condition is a specific situation that prevents an action from beginning or requires current work to halt. It is not a warning that can be averaged away.

Examples include having no accountable human owner, having no authorized person who can halt the work, using prohibited data or tools, being unable to validate a material output or being unable to contain and recover from a state-changing action.

When a stop condition applies, the product explains:

- the exact work and scope that must stop;
- what lower-risk work may continue;
- who owns the corrective action;
- what evidence will show that the correction works;
- who must verify it;
- which human decision is required before work may restart.

Stopping is not automatically the end of adoption. It prevents the organization from continuing under conditions it cannot defend. Verified remediation makes the scope eligible for reassessment. It does not automatically restore permission.

### Before you apply the playbook

**Heading**

Know what will happen before you enter information

**Copy**

Applying the playbook means using its decision structure for a specific organization, team, use case or adoption increment.

Before asking for information, the product will explain:

- the decision you are preparing to make;
- why the question matters;
- who owns the decision and who must participate;
- what evidence is expected;
- what favorable, unfavorable and unknown responses mean;
- what work may continue and what must stop;
- what record or report will be produced.

You will not be asked to fill in a blank workbook. Questions use structured choices where possible. When a written response is necessary, you will see a useful example, an insufficient example and an explanation of why they differ.

Unknown and not yet established are valid responses. Material unknowns create visible evidence gaps or restrictions. They are not treated as favorable answers.

### What to gather

**Heading**

Gather what you have. Missing information will remain visible.

**Introductory copy**

You do not need every artifact before choosing a starting point. Gather the records appropriate to the decision you selected. The product will identify missing material evidence and explain its consequence.

Useful information may include:

- the leadership request, business problem or delivery problem behind the work;
- the exact team, product, service, repository, work type and delivery activity being considered;
- the accountable sponsor, adoption lead and people who own affected product, engineering, security, quality, release, service and financial decisions;
- current delivery, review, test, security, release, operational and support records for comparable work;
- existing policies and approved boundaries for data, repositories, tools, credentials and environments;
- current cost information for labor, tools, infrastructure, integration, enablement, review, controls, rework and support;
- known incidents, defects, repeated failures, participant concerns and unresolved risks;
- records showing how work can be paused, access can be revoked and affected systems can be recovered.

Do not paste passwords, access tokens, private keys or unnecessary sensitive content into the workspace. Use an approved reference to protected evidence when the underlying record should remain in its system of record.

## Product-owner approval questions

Experience 1 is ready for approval only if the product owner agrees that:

1. The first visible content accurately describes why the intended reader arrives and what the product does.
2. The challenge descriptions use direct language and cover the enterprise questions that matter.
3. The nine-stage journey is understandable at this level without turning Home into a forced process.
4. The SDLC and participant sections prove that the product covers more than coding and cannot be owned by one function.
5. The evidence section sets the correct standard for tangible value, downstream effects and total cost.
6. Every starting choice makes the result, prerequisites and next action clear.
7. The workspace transition occurs only after purpose, consequences, participants and evidence are explained.
8. The starting-point summary is useful and is clearly distinguished from an approval or readiness result.
9. No reader-facing section is unnecessarily dense, vague or dependent on unexplained methodology.
10. This content is strong enough to become the first third of the Gate 6 vertical slice after Experiences 2 and 3 are approved.
